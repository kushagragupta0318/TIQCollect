# PTP and visit best practice

Guidance on structuring a Promise to Pay (PTP) and a field visit. Style and
approach only — the amount, date, and borrower facts in any generated text
must come from the case data, never from this file.

## What makes a PTP worth recording

A PTP is only useful to the next person who reads it if it is specific:
an exact amount, an exact date, and — where the borrower gave one — the
reason for the promise. "Will pay soon" is not a PTP a strategy can be
built on; "will pay ₹8,000 by the 15th because the salary credits on the
10th" is. A PTP taken on a visit should be followed up on or just after its
due date, not days later — a late follow-up reads to the borrower as the
promise not having mattered, which erodes the next one.

## Reading a broken PTP

A first broken PTP is not evidence of bad faith by itself — genuine
circumstances (a delayed salary, an emergency expense) break promises as
often as evasion does. What should change the strategy is a PATTERN: the
same borrower breaking consecutive promises with decreasing specificity
("will pay soon" each time) is a different case than one broken promise
followed by a kept one. A rescheduled PTP should carry a clear link to the
one it replaces, so the pattern stays visible to whoever reads the case
next.

## What a visit should establish before anything else

Before any negotiation, a visit should confirm three things: that this is
still the right person and address, whether the household's situation has
materially changed since the last contact (income, health, a dispute over
the loan), and what, if anything, the borrower can commit to today. A visit
that skips straight to negotiating an amount without establishing these
tends to produce promises that do not hold, because they were never
anchored to what the household can actually do.

## Visit cadence

Revisiting a household within a day or two of a kept promise, or
immediately after a borrower has explicitly asked for time, reads as
pressure rather than diligence and tends to harden a borrower's position
rather than move it. Spacing visits to match the borrower's own stated
timeline — while still visiting promptly when a promise is broken without
explanation — keeps the approach proportionate to what has actually
happened on the case.
